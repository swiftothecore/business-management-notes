Types of Businesses

Business structures available in Australia — ownership, liability, and the advantages and disadvantages of each.

Business Structure

🧑Sole Trader

  • Owned and operated by one person (may still have employees)
  • Owner makes most business decisions
  • Liability: unlimited - owner personally accountable for all debts
e.g. Owner invests $20k, business owes $50k - personal assets (home, car) can be seized to cover the debt
✔ Advantages
  • Easy and inexpensive to set up
  • Few legal/tax formalities
  • Owner has full control over assets and decisions
  • No obligation to disclose profits publicly
  • Easy to change structure or close down
✘ Disadvantages
  • Fully liable for business debts - personal assets at risk
  • Heavy reliance on owner - long work hours
  • Limited time away from the business (fewer holidays)
  • If owner is sick or injured, business may stop
Business Structure

🤝Partnership

  • Owned by 2-20 people
  • Liability: unlimited - all owners personally accountable for debts
  • Each partner liable for debts incurred by the others
✔ Advantages
  • Easy and inexpensive to set up
  • Combines resources and expertise of multiple people
  • More flexibility for holidays/sick leave than sole trader
  • No obligation to disclose profits publicly
✘ Disadvantages
  • Not a separate entity - all partners personally liable (unlimited liability)
  • Each partner liable for debts incurred by the others
  • Personal differences may interfere with business decisions
Business Structure

🏛️Private Limited Company

  • Separate legal entity - same rights as a natural person (can incur debt, sue, be sued)
  • 1-50 shareholders; shares sold privately - not listed on a stock exchange
  • Liability: limited - shareholders can only lose the value of their shares
e.g. Linfox, Wittner Shoes, Sportsgirl
✔ Advantages
  • Limited liability - shareholders can only lose share value
  • Tax advantages over sole traders and partnerships
  • Exists independently of shareholders - survives retirement/death
✘ Disadvantages
  • More complex - higher set-up and admin costs
  • More regulated than sole traders/partnerships
  • Cannot raise capital from the public (private shares only)
  • Suppliers/lenders may require personal guarantees from directors
Business Structure

📈Public Listed Company

  • Separate legal entity - same rights as a natural person (can incur debt, sue, be sued)
  • Shares sold to the public via the ASX; typically formed to raise/borrow public money
  • Shareholders: unrestricted number
  • Liability: limited - shareholders can only lose the value of their shares
e.g. Telstra, Commonwealth Bank (CBA), Wesfarmers
✔ Advantages
  • Limited liability - shareholders can only lose share value
  • Going public creates large capital influx without incurring debt
  • Can raise further funds by issuing more shares
  • Director personal guarantees not usually required for borrowing
  • ASX listing raises public awareness of the company
✘ Disadvantages
  • Complex structure - high set-up and admin costs
  • Must prioritise shareholder interests in decisions
  • Disclosure and reporting requirements apply (e.g. financial reports)
  • Share price vulnerable to market conditions regardless of performance
  • Original owners may lose control (run by board/managers)
  • Management may focus on short-term share price over long-term success
Business Type

🌿Social Enterprises

  • Exist to benefit the public/community - not shareholders or owners
  • Mission: economic, social, cultural or environmental
  • Sell goods/services and reinvest substantial profits into their mission
  • Not a legal structure - can be sole trader, partnership, etc.; structure chosen to best advance the mission
  • Liability depends on the chosen structure
  • Adv/disadv not covered in this course
e.g. The Big Issue, CERES Fair Food, Good Cycles
Business Type

🏦Government Business Enterprises (GBEs)

  • Government owned
  • Occupy strategic positions in Australia's economic and social life
  • Provide services the public market may not (communications, transport, defence)
  • Dual objective: provide a public service + make a profit
  • Profitability achieved by controlling costs and pricing goods/services accordingly
  • Not a structure entrepreneurs can choose - adv/disadv not covered in this course
e.g. Australia Post, NBN, Defence Housing Australia

Business Objectives

Common objectives businesses pursue — from making profit and growing market share to fulfilling social needs and meeting shareholder expectations.

Business Objective

💰To Make a Profit

  • Profit = revenue minus expenses
  • Most common business objective
  • Revenue = total income from sales
  • Expenses = costs of running the business
e.g. Lemonade stall: 50 cups × $2 = $100 revenue. Expenses: $5 cups + $20 lemons/sugar + $5 stall fee = $30. Profit = $70
Business Objective

📊To Increase Market Share

  • Market share = a business's portion of an industry's total sales, expressed as a %
  • Key indicator of how well a business is performing relative to competitors
  • To increase market share = gaining sales from competitors
  • Can only be achieved by capturing share from competitors - your slice grows, theirs shrinks
Analogy: the market is a cake; every business gets a slice. Growing your market share means your slice gets bigger while someone else's gets smaller.
Business Objective

⚙️To Improve Efficiency

  • Efficiency = minimising inputs used in the production process
  • Inputs to reduce: raw materials, labour, time, electricity, waste
  • Why: creates a cost or time advantage over competitors
e.g. Supermarket: self-service checkouts → fewer staff needed
e.g. Restaurant: correct ingredient quantities → less spoilage
e.g. Sportswear manufacturer: automated production → less time per item
e.g. Aldi has built its entire business model around efficiency
Business Objective

✅To Improve Effectiveness

  • Effectiveness = how well objectives have been met
  • Achieved by implementing strategies and comparing outcomes against set objectives
Signs a business has not achieved its objectives: defective products, high customer complaints, decreased market share
e.g. Chocolate manufacturer meeting taste/quality expectations → improved sales
e.g. Restaurant with correct ingredient quantities → fewer diner complaints
Business Objective

🎯To Fulfil a Market Need

  • Market need = product or service wanted/required by a specific group of customers
  • Applies to all businesses - without a market need, there are no customers
  • Identifying and fulfilling market needs allows businesses to grow, increase profit and market share
  • Ways to fulfil a market need:
    • Satisfying existing consumer demands
    • Filling a gap in the market (new product/service in a specific area)
    • Offering a new solution to an old problem
Business Objective

🤲To Fulfil a Social Need

  • Social need = product or service wanted or required within the community
  • Focus: common good of society/local community (not just profit)
  • Range: basic services (food, shelter) through to employment/education for disadvantaged groups
  • Closely linked to social enterprises (see Lesson 1)
e.g. Backpack Bed for Homeless: sells goods to the public, uses all profits to distribute bedding to homeless people - provides a bed every 2 hours
Business Objective

📋To Meet Shareholder Expectations

  • Shareholders = persons or groups who own shares in a company (i.e. the owners)
  • Shareholders benefit in two ways:
    • Dividends: a portion of net profit paid to shareholders
    • Capital gain: selling shares at a higher price than purchase price
  • Key expectations: high dividends + rising share prices
  • Businesses implement strategies specifically to meet these expectations
e.g. Commonwealth Bank paid shareholders a dividend of $1.75 per share (March 2022)

Strategies Related to Materials

How businesses plan, schedule, and manage the materials needed for production — from forecasting demand through to just-in-time ordering.

Materials Strategy

📈Forecasting

Predicting customer demand for upcoming periods using past data and market trends, so that production and material ordering can be planned accordingly.

  • Efficiency: Prevents both overstocking (high storage costs, wasted inputs) and understocking (production slowdowns)
  • Effectiveness: Helps the business meet demand, increasing customer satisfaction, market share, and profit
Limitations: Forecasts are not always 100% accurate. Gathering and analysing data can be time-consuming and costly.
Forecasting is often used in conjunction with a Master Production Schedule (MPS) and Materials Requirement Planning (MRP).
Materials Strategy

📅Master Production Schedule (MPS)

A detailed plan of what is to be produced and when it is to be produced.

  • Usually created using specialised software
  • Shows when the end product is needed — does not show when raw materials/components are needed (that is the role of MRP)
  • Aims to increase planning stability, provide information across the business, and reduce storage costs

👥Who uses the MPS?

  • Sales: Provides final product completion dates to promise products to customers
  • Inventory/Finance: Anticipates inventory levels of finished products
  • Operations: Drives the materials requirements planning process
Benefit: Improves effectiveness by helping the business consistently meet demand, without the chaos of responding to constantly changing orders in real time.
Materials Strategy

📋Materials Requirement Planning (MRP)

An itemised list of all the materials involved in production to meet the forecast demand.

  • Converts the MPS into a detailed purchasing list of raw materials and components
  • Usually generated using specialised software
  • Ensures the right materials are available at the right time, at the lowest possible cost

⚙️Benefits (Efficiency)

  • Prevents material shortages
  • Optimises cash flow
  • Automates the supply chain
Combined purpose of MPS and MRP: To guarantee availability of products, which satisfies customers and leads to increased profit and market share (effectiveness).
Materials Strategy

⚡Just-in-Time (JIT)

An approach to ordering materials where new materials arrive exactly when they are required in the production process — no sooner, no later.

✔ Advantages
  • Minimises storage costs (efficiency)
  • Less capital tied up in stock — freed up for other uses (efficiency)
  • Reduces the likelihood of materials being wasted due to damage or spoilage in storage (efficiency)
  • Lowers costs and increases profits (effectiveness)
✘ Disadvantages
  • Business becomes heavily dependent on suppliers — supply chain disruptions can cause production slowdowns or stops
  • Difficult to respond to sudden, unexpected increases in demand (insufficient materials on hand)
  • Little tolerance for mistakes, as minimal stock is kept for re-producing faulty products

Strategies Related to Quality

Three approaches businesses use to manage and improve the quality of their goods or services — from basic inspection through to a whole-business commitment to excellence.

Quality Strategy

🔍Quality Control

Involves the use of inspections at various points in the production process to check for problems and defects. If defects are found, corrective action is taken so that customers receive goods or services of high quality.

  • Establish quality standards
  • Carry out inspections at different stages of production
  • Compare results with set standards
  • Take corrective action if necessary
✔ Advantages
  • Low quality products are likely to be detected before reaching the customer
  • Simple to establish and inexpensive
  • Standards can be adjusted over time to meet changing conditions (e.g. rising customer expectations)
✘ Disadvantages
  • Reactive — by the time a fault is discovered, the low quality product has already been made
  • Takes time to conduct regular checks
Quality Strategy

🏅Quality Assurance

Involves an independent external business that audits against national or international standards. If standards are met, the business gains the right to display the external provider's logo.

e.g. International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO)
✔ Advantages
  • External standards prevent internal manipulation of results
  • Proactive — the goal is to avoid errors before they occur
  • Regular audits ensure continuous improvement
✘ Disadvantages
  • Can be time-consuming
  • Costs money for certification, implementation, and audits
  • Costs money to train staff
Quality Strategy

⭐Total Quality Management (TQM)

An ongoing, business-wide commitment to excellence shared by all employees. Aims to create a defect-free production process with a focus on the customer.

💪Employee Empowerment

Quality is the responsibility of every employee. Employee participation in decision-making encourages everyone to take ownership of quality, with each employee aiming to eliminate errors in their part of the process.

🔄Continuous Improvement

An ongoing commitment to becoming better in all aspects of the business, through constantly raising standards in pursuit of perfection.

🎯Customer Focus

Quality is defined by customer expectations. All employees must understand their target customers and consistently aim to satisfy their needs and wants.

✔ Advantages
  • All staff and management share ownership and responsibility for quality
  • Every aspect of the business is continually assessed
✘ Disadvantages
  • Can be difficult to allocate responsibility for errors
  • Relies on all staff being motivated and highly skilled
  • Requires strong communication skills to be effective

Lean Management

A production philosophy focused on eliminating waste by aligning output precisely to customer demand. Core principles: Pull, One-Piece Flow, Takt, and Zero Defects.

Lean Principle

←Pull

  • Production driven by actual customer demand - product is pulled backwards through manufacturing from the customer's order
  • Contrasts with push: push produces to forecasted demand, leaving goods in inventory until sold
  • Avoids overproduction and stockpiling, both of which create waste
  • Components replaced only once used - all resources go to goods that will actually sell
✔ Advantages
  • Low inventories - reduced storage costs and waste
  • Sale of produced units guaranteed (based on actual demand)
  • Suitable for unpredictable sales volumes
  • Increases flexibility - fast response to changes in demand
✘ Disadvantages
  • Heavily dependent on suppliers - supply chain disruptions can halt production entirely
Lean Principle

→One-Piece Flow

  • Only one item is at any given stage of production at one time
  • Contrasts with mass/batch production, where groups of items move together - inefficient due to task-switching and unnecessary movement
  • Multiple items can exist in the overall process simultaneously, but only one at each workstation
✔ Advantages
  • Errors detected earlier - focus on one item improves quality
  • Increased speed - eliminates waiting time and unnecessary movement
  • Reduces inventory costs
✘ Disadvantages
  • Expensive to reconfigure the production line
  • Employee motivation may decrease due to monotonous, repetitive tasks
  • Not suitable for high-variety products
  • Not suitable for products requiring batch processing
Lean Principle

⏱Takt

  • Takt time = the rate at which a business must complete a product to meet customer demand
  • Applies to both manufacturing and service businesses
Formula: Takt time = Available production time ÷ Units required
⚠ Slower than takt time
  • Backlog occurs - customer demand cannot be met
⚠ Faster than takt time
  • Staff and machinery underutilised
  • Producing more than needed causes overproduction
Lean Principle

✦Zero Defects

  • "Do it right the first time" - avoid and reduce errors to prevent costly corrections later
  • Defects found at one stage must be detected before passing to the next
✔ Advantages
  • Reduces cost and waste
  • Increases customer satisfaction - potentially improves sales and profit
  • Creates a culture of continuous improvement
✘ Disadvantages
  • Unrealistic to expect zero defects entirely
  • Dedicating excessive resources to it can be a poor use of those resources
  • Increased pressure on staff can negatively impact morale
Week 14 · Lesson 2

Global Considerations

Strategies businesses use to operate beyond their home country — sourcing inputs globally, manufacturing overseas, and outsourcing functions to foreign businesses.

Global Strategy

🌐Global Sourcing of Inputs

  • Obtaining inputs (raw materials, parts) from suppliers outside the home country
✔ Advantages
  • Lower costs: overseas manufacturing costs often cheaper → reduces prices, increases competitiveness and profit (efficiency + effectiveness)
  • Access to inputs not available locally: parts, machinery, equipment, world-class technology, specialised knowledge
  • Overseas inputs may be of higher quality than local alternatives
✘ Disadvantages
  • Supply chain interruptions: inputs may not arrive as planned → slows or stops production; backup plans required
  • Quality harder to monitor: if inputs don't meet standard, replacement takes longer due to shipping times
  • Must ensure suppliers meet CSR and OH&S expectations — fair wages, safe workplaces, environmental protection
Global Strategy

🏭Overseas Manufacture

  • Production of a good located in a different country to the business's headquarters
✔ Advantages
  • Lower costs: labour and production costs cheaper overseas, government regulations often less strict (= efficiency) → lower prices, greater competitiveness and profit (= effectiveness)
  • If products are sold overseas, manufacturing locally to that market can speed up delivery
✘ Disadvantages
  • Jobs lost domestically: impacts employees, families and community; damages business reputation; customers may prefer locally made products
  • If outputs are sold back home: shipping costs, timing and interruptions must be managed; shipping also has an environmental impact
  • Significant initial set-up costs; obtaining permits from foreign governments is time intensive and expensive
Global Strategy

🔗Global Outsourcing

  • Transferring some business functions to external, overseas businesses
  • Business runs a tender and selects another business to complete a specific task
  • Typically done to reduce costs, improve quality, or improve productivity
e.g. Website development outsourced to Asia (lower labour costs); call centres for telecoms/finance outsourced to India or the Philippines
✔ Advantages
  • Tender process allows selection of the best available business at the best price
  • Outsourcing to specialists frees the business to focus on core activities
  • If the relationship fails, ending the contract is simpler than closing overseas manufacturing (no own staff hired, no HR process required)
✘ Disadvantages
  • Loss of control over the production process; sensitive business information must be shared with third parties
  • Language and cultural barriers can cause issues
  • Time intensive to find the right partner business

Stakeholders

Stakeholders are individuals or groups with a vested or direct interest in a business. The Study Design lists six types. Their differing interests can lead to conflict.

Definition

👥Stakeholders

  • Individuals or groups with a vested or direct interest in a business
  • Can affect and/or be affected by the operations of a business
  • Their support is necessary for a business to be successful
Study tip: It is not enough to say stakeholders have an "interest" — you must say vested or direct interest. In answers, you need to identify who the stakeholder is, what their specific interest is, and how their interests may conflict with another stakeholder's.
The Six Types

📋Six Stakeholders at a Glance

🏦Owners

Legal proprietors; interested in profit, high dividends, and rising share prices.

💼Managers

Employed by owners to direct the business; interested in high pay, job security, and decision-making involvement.

👤Employees

Work for the business; interested in fair pay, safe conditions, job security, and advancement.

🛒Customers

Purchase goods and services; want fair prices, quality, and ethical business behaviour.

🚚Suppliers

Provide goods and services to the business; interested in fair prices, timely payment, and long-term contracts.

🏘️General Community

People in the area where the business operates; interested in environmental protection, local jobs, and minimising disruption.

Stakeholder

🏦Owners

  • The legal proprietors of the business
  • The number of owners depends on the type of business (e.g. a partnership can have 2–20 owners)
  • Interested in the business succeeding and making a profit
  • Shareholders specifically want high dividends and for share prices to go up
Stakeholder

💼Managers

  • Employed by the owners to direct the business
  • Interested in: high pay, ongoing employment, being involved in decisions, achieving improvements in performance indicators (e.g. profit)
e.g. Qantas Chief Executive Officer (CEO) Vanessa Hudson.
Stakeholder

👤Employees

  • People who work for the business in exchange for a salary or wage
  • Interested in: fair pay, good and safe working conditions, job security, prospects for advancement, access to suitable training, work-life balance
e.g. Shop assistants and other staff who work for Myer.
Stakeholder

🛒Customers

  • Individuals and groups who purchase goods and services from the business
  • Interested in: fair prices, good quality, value for money, product availability, customer service
  • Want the actions of the business to align with their personal ethics
Stakeholder

🚚Suppliers

  • Provide goods and services to the business
  • Interested in: fair prices, being paid on time, securing long-term contracts
e.g. Farmers who supply fresh fruit and vegetables to supermarkets.
Stakeholder

🏘️General Community

  • The people living in the geographical area where the business is located or operates
  • Interested in: protection of the environment; provision of local jobs; general local impact (e.g. traffic congestion, building of infrastructure)
Key Concept

⚡Potential Conflicts Between Stakeholders

Stakeholders have quite different interests, which can lead to potential conflicts.

Important: The conflict of interests is between two stakeholders — not between a stakeholder and the business.
Example

💸Employees vs Owners: Pay Rise Example

  • Employees are granted a pay rise
  • This leads to overall higher expenses and lower profit for the business
  • Owners/shareholders may receive lower dividends as a result
  • This creates a potential conflict between employees (who benefit from the pay rise) and owners (who are disadvantaged by lower dividends)
Exam Technique

📝How to Identify a Conflict in an Answer

  • Identify one stakeholder who is against a proposed change or decision
  • Identify one stakeholder who is in favour of it
  • Explain why each stakeholder holds that position — link to their specific interests

Management Skills

Management skills are the abilities and attributes a manager needs to run a business successfully. They can be learnt and gained through experience and training.

Management Skill

💬Communication

  • The process of creating and exchanging information between people that produces feedback
  • Feedback = the response or reaction of the receiver after perceiving or understanding the message; enables the sender to evaluate the effectiveness of the message; necessary to make two-way communication complete and effective
  • Managers use communication to: provide information to internal or external stakeholders; clarify tasks; have discussions and negotiations

Types of communication:

  • Verbal (written) - manager expresses ideas through documents
  • Verbal (oral) - manager speaks directly to employees and gains feedback
  • Non-verbal - body language and visuals
e.g. Louisa (manager at Wonderful Icecreams) asks product developer Jean to develop a new coconut milk summer flavour. Jean's feedback: "OK. I'll get started straight away."
Management Skill

📤Delegation

  • The transfer of authority and responsibility from a manager to an employee to carry out specific activities
  • The manager often provides guidelines (e.g. instructions) or limitations (e.g. a budget)
  • Occurs at all levels: senior manager → middle manager; front-line manager → worker
✔ Benefits for the Manager
  • Time is freed up to focus on higher-level tasks
  • Reduced workload can lower stress
  • Useful for succession planning — develops someone who can step up if the manager leaves or is promoted
✔ Benefits for the Employee
  • Given more responsibility and can develop skills and gain valuable experience
  • Enhances prospects for promotion
  • Can increase motivation and morale
Key Concept

✅Suitable vs Unsuitable Tasks for Delegation

✔ Suitable
  • Routine, repetitive or time-consuming tasks
  • Tasks good for training and developing subordinates
✘ Unsuitable
  • High risk, high cost, or crisis-related tasks
  • Confidential or strategically important tasks
Key Concept

⚠️Reasons for Poor Delegation

  • Concern that the task will not be performed to the expected standard — lack of trust in employees
  • Too busy to delegate; believes it is faster to do the job themselves
  • Fear that a subordinate may perform the task better — feels threatened in their role
  • Regards the task as part of their personal authority or status
  • Subordinates lack the training and experience needed before delegation is possible
Management Skill

🗺️Planning

  • The process of setting objectives and developing strategies to achieve those objectives
  • All plans must be aligned with the business's vision and mission statement
  • All levels of management carry out planning, each at a different timescale
Planning Levels

📊Three Levels of Planning

  • Strategic planning (Senior managers) — long-term goals spanning 2–5 years; apply to the entire business; set by senior management; establish the overall direction; deemed of high importance to future success
    • e.g. Expanding into new markets over the next 5 years; developing a new product group over the next 4 years
  • Tactical planning (Middle managers) — medium-term goals spanning 1–2 years; often department-level objectives; provide more detailed direction within the strategic plan
    • e.g. Forecasting a 10% increase in sales over the next 18 months; conducting a customer needs analysis
  • Operational planning (Front-line managers) — short-term goals ranging from daily to up to 1 year; set by lower management; apply to their immediate team
    • e.g. Setting the number of units to be produced per day; implementing minor changes to the production process
Key Concept

🔗Alignment of Objectives

  • Every objective within the organisation must be aligned with the higher objectives and the mission/vision statement
  • Strategic objectives only become useful when translated into tactical and operational objectives
  • Operational objectives are only effective when designed to serve the tactical and strategic objectives
e.g. CEO sets strategic objective: Increase profit by 30% over the next 5 years → Operations Manager sets tactical objective: Decrease faulty product returns by 10% within 1 year → Production Supervisor sets operational objective: Provide training to staff about quality standards
Management Skill

🚩Leadership

  • The process of guiding, influencing and motivating employees to achieve the business's objectives
  • A manager with strong leadership skills has a positive impact on staff motivation and productivity
  • Leaders are more than just managers — they often have inspirational qualities that allow them to promote and share a vision; this generates motivation and loyalty, and can create a positive workplace culture

Examples of effective leadership include the ability to:

  • Motivate individuals and groups
  • Reach goals and achieve results
  • Think strategically and have 'helicopter' vision — seeing how all parts of the organisation connect (likened to looking down at the whole environment from above)
  • Set work performance standards such as output expectations, overall work requirements, and teamwork
Management Skill

🔀Decision-making

  • The ability to select the best option from a range of choices to solve problems or take up opportunities
  • All levels of management make decisions — top management (business expansion, financial strategy, product mix), middle managers (obtaining and organising resources), front-line managers (technical and practical decisions such as output levels)
  • A logical and systematic approach is recommended — the IGDACE process
Exam tip: Examiners have considered 'problem solving' and 'decision-making' as the same thing. The IGDACE process applies to both — every step makes sense whether solving a problem or making a decision.
Process

📋The IGDACE Decision-making Process

  • I — Identify the decision to be made — clearly define what decision must be made
  • G — Gather information — investigate circumstances and search for factors that may affect the decision
  • D — Develop alternatives — generate several possible options, including creative and imaginative solutions
  • A — Analyse alternatives — examine and rank options; assess advantages/disadvantages, strengths/weaknesses, and costs/benefits
  • C — Choose an alternative and implement it — select the preferred option and put it into effect
  • E — Evaluate the implementation — review progress and make necessary adjustments
Management Skill

🤝Interpersonal Skills

  • The ability to deal and interact with people and build positive relationships
  • Very important as managers achieve objectives through other people
  • A manager with strong interpersonal skills has a positive impact on staff motivation and productivity

Qualities include:

  • Ability to work effectively in a team
  • Being a good communicator
  • Ability to motivate and encourage others
  • Being honest and respectful of other values, beliefs and approaches
  • Ability to relate to diverse people (different cultures, ages, genders)
  • Showing empathy
Study tip: If asked to select two management skills, choose skills that are clearly different from each other. Communication and interpersonal can sound very similar when applied to a scenario — choosing both risks losing marks.

Management Styles

Management style refers to the behaviour and attitude of the manager towards employees — how they communicate, make decisions, and give direction. Five styles are studied in this course.

Definition

👔Management Style

  • The behaviour and attitude of the manager towards employees
  • The way in which managers communicate, make decisions, and give direction
  • There is no single 'best' or 'worst' management style — the appropriate style depends on the situation
  • Factors that influence the choice of style: nature of the task, time available, experience and skill levels of employees, and the manager's own preference
The Five Styles

📊Five Management Styles at a Glance

Style Decision-making Communication Delegation
Autocratic Centralised One-way None
Persuasive Centralised One-way (with explanation) None
Consultative Centralised Two-way None
Participative Decentralised Two-way High
Laissez-faire Decentralised Minimal Full
Management Style

👑Autocratic

The manager solely makes all decisions and tells staff what has been decided. Employees have no input.

Characteristics:

  • Decision-making: centralised — manager makes all decisions and passes directions to subordinates
  • Delegation: none — the manager does not delegate decision-making
  • Communication: one-way (top-down) — employees have no say

Suitable situations:

  • Times of crisis or risk — manager can take charge and make decisions quickly in the best interest of the business (e.g. product recalls, negative news stories); no employee input needed so decisions are made rapidly
  • Difficult or high-importance decisions (e.g. closure of a location, retrenchment of staff)
  • When employees lack experience and knowledge and require clear, specific and immediate direction
✔ Advantages
  • Direct and clear communication — all communication from one source (the manager) ensures consistency in strategy and decision-making; little chance of misunderstandings or inconsistencies
  • Fast — decisions made quickly as only one person is responsible
✘ Disadvantages
  • Lack of employee input — creates low satisfaction and morale; employees frustrated their ideas are not heard; leads to a poor manager-staff relationship
  • Ignores valuable insights workers have into business operations
  • Causes division — subordinates not encouraged to work together or with the manager; creates an 'us v them' attitude
Management Style

🗣️Persuasive

Very similar to the autocratic style. The key difference is that the manager provides more information to employees about how and why decisions were made — the manager makes the decisions and then 'sells' them to staff.

Characteristics:

  • Decision-making: centralised — manager makes all decisions and passes directions to subordinates
  • Delegation: none — the manager does not delegate decision-making
  • Communication: one-way (top-down), but the manager explains the rationale for decisions; persuasive managers spend time convincing subordinates the decisions are correct by mapping out the benefits

Suitable situations:

  • Times of crisis or risk — can make decisions quickly without seeking employee input (e.g. product recall, negative news stories)
  • When employees lack experience and knowledge
  • When co-operation from employees is required, but not their input
✔ Advantages
  • Direct and clear communication — from one source; ensures consistency; little misunderstanding
  • Fast — only one person makes decisions
  • Higher subordinate acceptance — while the manager makes all decisions, employees are more likely to accept them as the manager has persuaded them on the merits and rationale
✘ Disadvantages
  • Lack of employee input — low satisfaction and morale; employees frustrated; poor relationship
  • Ignores valuable insights from workers
  • Causes division — 'us v them' attitude between workers and management
Management Style

💬Consultative

The manager takes into account the opinions of employees before making a decision. Unlike autocratic and persuasive styles, the manager does consider employee views — but still makes the final decision themselves.

Characteristics:

  • Decision-making: centralised — manager makes decisions and passes directions to subordinates
  • Delegation: none — the manager does not delegate decision-making
  • Communication: two-way — the manager seeks employees' opinions before deciding; feedback and discussions are encouraged

Suitable situations:

  • During times of change — to decrease uncertainty and increase motivation by involving employees in the process
  • When problem solving is required
  • When employees have experience and knowledge
✔ Advantages
  • Employees' ideas are heard and may improve the decision-making process
  • Opportunity to explore a greater variety of ideas
  • Higher morale and motivation — employees are involved in the process
✘ Disadvantages
  • More time-consuming than autocratic or persuasive — consulting adds time before deciding
  • Resentment possible if employees' ideas are not implemented
  • Not consulting every time may lead to employee frustration
  • Employees may not understand the complexity of the issue
Management Style

🤝Participative

Management and employees make decisions together — employees actively participate. Usually not applied to all decisions; typically used for middle- and lower-level decisions.

Characteristics:

  • Decision-making: decentralised — managers and employees make decisions together
  • Delegation: managers delegate some authority; employees can be given responsibility for decisions in their own area of responsibility; corporate objectives are still set by senior management
  • Communication: two-way — employees provide opinions and feedback

Suitable situations:

  • During times of change
  • When problem solving is required
  • When creativity is required
  • Works well for middle- or lower-level decisions, particularly when issues directly impact employees
  • When employees are experienced and knowledgeable
✔ Advantages
  • Strong relationships form between manager and employees, with a high level of trust in employees' abilities
  • There is a larger pool of ideas
  • High motivation — employees have responsibility for the decision-making process
  • Employees feel a sense of ownership and empowerment
  • Employees are more receptive to change as they have been involved in the actual decision-making
✘ Disadvantages
  • More time-consuming than consultative — reaching agreement takes longer than just taking ideas into account
  • Manager's role can be undermined if they are not involved in decision-making in a specific area
  • Can cause conflict between employees when viewpoints differ or the abilities of co-workers are questioned
  • Employees may not want to be involved in decision-making and may resent this style
Management Style

🕊️Laissez-faire

The manager gives full responsibility for decision-making to employees. A 'hands-off' approach where the manager does not intervene in employees' areas of responsibility. Employees accomplish tasks with very little guidance. Managers are typically only involved in higher-level tasks — setting budgets and timelines, and ensuring overall objectives are met.

Exam note: You might recognise this style in a case study, but think carefully before recommending it as a suitable style.

Characteristics:

  • Decision-making: decentralised — employees make decisions; manager is only involved if asked
  • Delegation: authority and responsibility for day-to-day running is fully delegated to employees; management is responsible for setting overall objectives and monitoring overall performance
  • Communication: two-way and completely open

Suitable situations:

  • With highly skilled employees who are experts in their area and capable of working independently
  • When employees are passionate and highly motivated
  • When high creativity is required
  • Often found in start-up businesses, advertising agencies, and architectural or engineering firms
✔ Advantages
  • A sense of ownership and increases in motivation and job satisfaction are possible
  • Encourages creativity and innovation
  • Communication is completely open
✘ Disadvantages
  • Loss of control by management over the decision-making process
  • Employees may feel lost due to lack of guidance
  • Could lead to misuse of resources (time and costs)
  • Can cause conflict among employees when opinions or approaches differ and no manager is present to help resolve it
  • Can lead to loss of focus on overall objectives

Corporate Culture

Corporate culture is the shared values and beliefs within a business. It shapes behaviour, affects morale and motivation, and has a powerful impact on achieving business objectives.

Definition

🏛️Corporate Culture

  • The shared values and beliefs within a business
  • Determines how people behave and act towards each other — what is acceptable and unacceptable behaviour
  • All businesses have their own corporate culture, developed and formed over time — both intentionally and unintentionally
  • Affects the way people act, and therefore has a powerful impact on achieving business objectives
  • Affects morale, motivation and retention
  • Businesses aim to develop a positive and desired corporate culture
🏢 Official Corporate Culture

The desired culture the business wants to have. Observed in:

  • Mission statement
  • Value statement
  • Advertising, slogans, symbols and logos
  • Dress code
  • Physical environment
👥 Real Corporate Culture

The actual culture that exists within the business. Observed in:

  • Actual behaviour
  • Management style
  • Language
  • Rituals, rites and celebrations
Corporate Culture Type

🏢Official Corporate Culture

The desired culture that the business wants to have. Can be seen and observed in:

  • Mission statement - a formal statement that describes a business's purpose and overall intention
  • Value statement - a written statement that outlines the core principles of what is important in that business (e.g. teamwork, customer service, innovation)
  • Advertising, slogans, symbols and logos - can represent and summarise the business's culture (e.g. Woolworths promotes the slogan 'The fresh food people')
  • Dress code - a written set of rules stipulating what type of clothing employees are allowed to wear; can include a uniform, formal or casual clothing
  • Physical environment - open-plan offices foster teamwork, whereas closed offices are less likely to do so
Corporate Culture Type

👥Real Corporate Culture

The actual culture that exists within the business. Can be seen and observed in:

  • Actual behaviour - how employees treat each other, and how they interact with customers and suppliers
  • Management style - shows the actual relationship between managers and staff
  • Language - can show the degree of formality within a business (e.g. are first names used, or titles such as Mr/Mrs?)
  • Rituals, rites and celebrations - provide a sense of belonging and acknowledge what is important in the business (e.g. shared morning teas, birthday celebrations)
Key distinction: The official culture is what the business wants; the real culture is what actually exists. Businesses aim to close the gap between the two.

Human Resource Management & Workplace Relations

Human resource management covers how a business manages people and their employment relationship. Workplace relations involves five key participants working together to determine wages and conditions.

Definition

👥Human Resource Management (HRM)

  • The management of people within the business and their employment relationship
  • Includes: recruiting, training and motivating employees; managing their performance; and managing the end of the employment relationship
  • Employees are the human resources a business needs to achieve its objectives — they are the people completing necessary work tasks
  • Employees are a key component of a business's competitive advantage; when managed well, a business is more likely to achieve its objectives
Workplace Relations

🤝What is Workplace Relations?

  • The system that determines wages and conditions of work, involving five key participants
  • Aims to get all parties working together to achieve productivity gains for the business while providing employees with good pay and working conditions
  • The goal is an optimum working relationship between employees and employers — leading to motivated and effective employees, and increases in productivity, competitiveness and profit
  • Even in an optimal system, conflicts of interest exist: employees want higher wages, better conditions and more leave; all of this increases costs, which works against the employer's interest of lower costs and higher profit
The Five Participants

📋Five Participants in Workplace Relations

👤Employees

Work in exchange for remuneration; interested in fair pay, safe conditions, job security and advancement.

✊Unions

Represent employees' interests during bargaining and disputes.

💼HR Managers

Represent the employer in negotiations, implement agreements, and manage disputes.

🏢Employer Associations

Represent employers' interests — the employer equivalent of unions.

⚖️Fair Work Commission

Australia's national workplace relations tribunal; independent regulator and umpire.

Participant

👤Employees

  • People who work in exchange for remuneration, usually in the form of a wage or salary
  • Interested in: fair pay, good and safe working conditions, job security, prospects for advancement, access to suitable training, and work-life balance
  • General role: work towards achieving business objectives
  • Not all employees are union members — only those who have joined and pay membership fees
Enterprise Agreements

📝Employees and Enterprise Agreements

  • In businesses that use enterprise agreements, employees have a more active role in workplace relations — enterprise agreements are negotiated at the workplace level
  • Employees need to vote on the terms and conditions the proposed agreement contains
  • Employees may also vote for or against industrial action (e.g. a strike)
Participant

💼Human Resource Managers

HR managers fulfil several tasks in relation to workplace relations:

  • Negotiation - represent the employer in negotiating new agreements; organise the time and resources required for the process (often involving long discussions with employees and/or unions); ensure agreements align with the business's financial situation, overall objectives, and legal requirements
  • Implementation - ensure agreements are implemented correctly; organise new wage levels, leave arrangements, or other conditions; update paperwork such as job descriptions; keep up to date with legal requirements and work agreements (e.g. minimum wage, annual leave entitlements); ensures a positive employer-employee relationship
  • Disputes - if negotiations fail and industrial action occurs (e.g. a strike), the HR manager is heavily involved in resolving the dispute; the aim is to get employees back to work as soon as possible to restore productivity; must have knowledge of workplace relations legislation
Participant

✊Unions

A union is an association that represents the interests of employees in a particular industry or occupation. The union:

  • Represents employees during bargaining negotiations
  • Acts as official spokesperson to the media on behalf of its members
  • Organises industrial action (e.g. a strike) as part of the bargaining process
  • Provides information and assistance to individual workers
  • Works with management to help resolve workplace issues
  • Looks into suspected breaches of workplace laws, discrimination laws, and workplace safety laws
Note: Not all employees are union members. Only employees who have joined a union and pay membership fees are members of that union.
Participant

🏢Employer Associations

Employer associations represent employers in workplace relations matters — they are the equivalent of unions, but on the employer's side. Employer associations:

  • Represent employers during the enterprise bargaining process
  • Provide legal advice to employers
  • Act as a spokesperson for their members as a bloc
  • Lobby governments to influence policy decisions
  • Research, inform and educate members on industry trends and issues
e.g. The National Retail Association (NRA) is one of Australia's largest and most representative retail industry organisations. It represents the interests of the retail, fast food and broader service sector, delivering critical information and advice to thousands of businesses nationally.
Participant

⚖️The Fair Work Commission (FWC)

  • Australia's national workplace relations tribunal
  • An independent government organisation that regulates Australia's workplace relations system
  • Ensures the relationship between employees and employers is fair and productive

Responsible for:

  • Setting minimum wages
  • Creating, reviewing and varying awards
  • Approving agreements
  • Acting as an independent umpire for disputes (e.g. unfair dismissal, workplace bullying)

Methods of Determining Wages and Conditions

The VCE Study Design identifies two methods for determining wages and conditions of employment: awards and agreements. Most employees are covered by one or the other.

Method

🏅Awards

  • Cover a whole industry or occupation and provide a safety net of minimum pay rates and employment conditions
  • Apply to all employers and employees who perform work covered by the award
  • Determined, reviewed and modified by the Fair Work Commission
  • Awards do not apply when a business has an enterprise agreement in place

Awards contain terms and conditions about:

  • Minimum wages
  • Overtime rates
  • Types of employment (e.g. full-time, part-time, casual)
  • Work arrangements (e.g. rosters, variation to working hours)
  • Hours of work
  • Rest breaks
  • Leave
e.g. Jo runs a building and construction business with qualified carpenters and office administration staff. Two awards apply: the Building and Construction Award (for carpenters) and the Clerks Award (for office staff). Other examples include the Fast Food Industry Award and the General Retail Industry Award.
✔ Advantages
  • Provides minimum conditions and pay rates across an entire industry or occupation — transparency and security for employees; rates cannot be reduced by the employer
  • Time-efficient — no negotiation is necessary
✘ Disadvantages
  • Less flexibility to cater for the specific requirements of individual workplaces and employees
Method

📄Enterprise Agreements

  • Set employment conditions for a business or a group of businesses (e.g. Woolworths Supermarkets Agreement 2018, KFC National Enterprise Agreement 2020)
  • Allow a business to develop a tailored set of employment conditions for their workers, which may better cover the particular needs of the business
  • Allow employees to negotiate 'above-award' pay and conditions
  • Once negotiations are concluded, the agreement must be submitted to the Fair Work Commission for approval before it can commence operation
Reminder: Awards do not apply when a business has an enterprise agreement in place.
Key Concept

✅Better Off Overall Test (BOOT)

  • All employment terms in an enterprise agreement must meet the better off overall test
  • Employees covered by an enterprise agreement cannot receive fewer entitlements than those set out in the applicable award
Process

🤝Enterprise Bargaining

  • An enterprise agreement is negotiated between the employer and a representative of the employees — this process is called bargaining
  • A key rule is 'bargaining in good faith' — both sides must play fair, which includes: attending and participating at meetings; disclosing relevant information in a timely manner; giving genuine consideration to proposals
  • Once negotiations are concluded, the agreement is submitted to the Fair Work Commission for approval before it can come into operation
✔ Advantages
  • Greater flexibility — workplace-specific matters can be catered for
  • Easier to link wage growth with productivity increases
  • Parties are directly involved in negotiations — the agreement is not imposed by a third party; can increase commitment and motivation
  • Employees must be better off than under the award (advantage for employees)
✘ Disadvantages
  • Time consuming to negotiate and go through the FWC approval process
  • Generally more expensive for employers — must pass the better off overall test
  • Leads to less wage equality and transparency across an industry

Dispute Resolution

Disputes can arise in any workplace. Managing them effectively through mediation or arbitration helps maintain good employee relationships and business productivity.

Definition

⚠️What is a Dispute?

  • A dispute exists when one or more people disagree about something and the matter remains unresolved
  • Can arise in any workplace; often concern equal opportunity, occupational health and safety (including bullying), and more recently privacy issues
  • Dispute resolution refers to the processes by which disputes are ended; two methods are studied: mediation and arbitration
e.g. An employee who feels bullied by their direct supervisor.
Why It Matters

🤝Value of Effective Dispute Resolution

  • Helps employers maintain good relationships with employees by dealing with workplace issues at an early stage
  • Employees are more likely to be cooperative and productive if they know that their grievances will be taken seriously
  • Provides the opportunity for an independent party to assist if the dispute cannot be resolved at the workplace level
  • It is preferable to resolve disputes at the workplace level first — this saves time and costs and avoids the stress of a formal legal process
Dispute Resolution Method

🕊️Mediation

  • A dispute resolution method where a neutral third party (the mediator) facilitates a dialogue to help the parties reach a conclusive and mutually satisfactory agreement
  • The mediator does not decide the outcome — the parties jointly come to the agreement themselves
  • An informal, confidential and voluntary process
  • One of the processes used by the Fair Work Commission to facilitate dispute resolution
  • Preferred over arbitration as a first step after workplace-level resolution fails
✔ Advantages
  • The parties themselves come to the agreement — increases satisfaction with and acceptance of the decision
  • Requires parties to work together, which can lead to an improvement of their relationship
  • The process is confidential and voluntary
✘ Disadvantages
  • No formal rules — if one party is more aggressive, they can advance their interests further at the expense of the other
  • Time consuming and expensive, with no requirement for a solution at the end (can conclude without an agreement)
Dispute Resolution Method

⚖️Arbitration

  • Parties refer their dispute to an arbitrator, who reviews the evidence, listens to all sides, and then makes a decision if the parties cannot reach an agreement on their own
  • The arbitrator's decision is binding by law
  • Less formal, less expensive, and faster than a courtroom hearing or trial — but more formal than mediation
✔ Advantages
  • Always ends with a final, legally binding decision
  • Less formal, less expensive and faster than a courtroom hearing or trial
  • Parties agree in advance to be bound by the arbitrator's decision
✘ Disadvantages
  • More formal than mediation
  • Transfer of decision-making authority to the arbitrator — possible loss of control over the outcome
  • Parties may not be satisfied with the decision
Comparison

📊Mediation vs Arbitration

🕊️ Mediation ⚖️ Arbitration
Decision / Judgement Made by agreement between both parties. The mediator does not make the decision. If parties do not reach an agreement, the arbitrator makes a legally binding decision.
Cost Usually less expensive — lower mediator fees, no evidence presentation required, and a faster process. Generally more costly — higher arbitrator fees, evidence must be presented and evaluated before a ruling is made.
Outcome Ends when an agreement is reached or when parties are deadlocked — mediation can end without reaching an agreement. There is always a decision at the end of arbitration.

Motivation Theories

Motivation theories explain what drives people to put effort into their work. Three theories are studied in this course, each approaching motivation from a different perspective.

Definition

🧠What is Motivation?

  • Motivation: the desire or drive to achieve an objective; the willingness of a person to expend energy and effort in doing a job or task
  • Theory: an explanation for a phenomenon, capable of predicting future occurrences and capable of being tested or falsified through empirical observation
  • Motivation theories: try to explain what motivates people and how this process happens; three specific theories are studied in this course, each approaching motivation from a different perspective
  • Knowledge of motivation theories gives managers insight into human behaviour and ideas and strategies for motivating employees
Key Concept

✅Benefits of Motivated Employees

A business benefits when employees are motivated. Motivated employees:

  • Are more effective and productive
  • Have a lower rate of absenteeism
  • Have higher job satisfaction — which usually means a lower rate of staff turnover (annual percentage of staff that leave their job)
  • Make a positive impact on customers; fewer customer complaints
  • Contribute to an increase in the quality of products
  • Learn faster and have more ideas
  • Are more committed to the long-term success of the business
Motivation Theory

🔺Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

  • Published in 1943 by psychologist Abraham Maslow
  • Proposes that within all individuals exist five hierarchical needs, arranged by importance
  • Most basic needs (physiological and security) are at the bottom; higher-level needs (social, esteem, self-actualisation) are at the top
  • Main idea: humans are motivated by unsatisfied needs; basic needs must be satisfied before higher-level needs can be addressed
The Five Levels

📊The Five Needs (Top to Bottom)

  • 5. Self-actualisation - realising personal potential, personal growth and self-fulfilment
    • Employees: opportunities to be challenged and creative; personal growth and advancement; participation in decision-making
  • 4. Self-esteem - external needs (status, respect, attention) and internal needs (self-respect, achievement, independence)
    • Employees: good reputation, public recognition, job title and responsibilities, promotion, rewards/bonus
  • 3. Love and belonging - friendship, affection and acceptance
    • Employees: good relationships with colleagues and management; acceptance from team; organised activities (e.g. birthday celebrations, meals)
  • 2. Safety and security - protection from physical and emotional harm
    • Employees: job security, safe and healthy working conditions, superannuation and insurance
  • 1. Physiological needs - basic needs (food, water, air, shelter, sleep, being well)
    • Employees: a job; remuneration (pay)
✔ Advantages
  • Easily understood and applied; gives managers an understanding of how employees are motivated so they can create workplaces that satisfy those needs
  • Holistic approach — considers physical, financial, emotional, social and intellectual aspects of motivation
✘ Disadvantages
  • Assumes everyone is motivated in the prescribed hierarchical order (e.g. how would artists fit?)
  • No evidence that a need ceases to be a motivator once fulfilled (e.g. someone seeking challenge still wants a safe environment)
  • No explanation for what motivates people once self-actualisation has been achieved
  • Does not account for other variables — an employee's position in the business, age, sex, cultural background, or social and economic circumstances
Application

⚙️How Does Maslow's Theory Work?

  • Physiological needs are the most important and must be satisfied first; once satisfied, the employee moves up to safety, then love and belonging, and so on
  • One does not feel the second need until the first has been satisfied
  • A need is a motivating factor as long as it remains unsatisfied — it dominates thoughts and influences attitude and behaviour at work
  • Once a need is satisfied, it is no longer a motivator
Application

👔How Can a Manager Use This Theory?

  • Identify where staff are on the hierarchy and implement strategies to satisfy those needs, therefore motivating employees
  • Employees will likely be at different stages — recent graduates will be near the bottom (basic needs); experienced employees may be higher (self-esteem, self-actualisation); many different strategies will be needed
e.g. After workplace accidents, employees are likely at the safety level. A manager could arrange equipment-handling training or purchase safer equipment to satisfy this need and allow employees to progress up the hierarchy.
Motivation Theory

🎯Goal Setting Theory (Locke and Latham)

  • Developed by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham
  • Based on the argument that motivation is derived from effective goal setting — working towards a goal provides motivation and improves performance
  • Five fundamental principles should be applied when setting goals for employees
The Five Principles

📋The Five Principles of Effective Goal Setting

  • Clarity - goals must be specific, measurable, and unambiguous; when employees fully understand what is expected, they can direct all energy into doing the work, increasing motivation
  • Challenge - goals must be difficult but not overwhelming; if too difficult or unrealistic, employees are more likely to give up; challenging goals mobilise energy and lead to higher effort
  • Commitment - goals must be understood and agreed to in order to be effective; employees are more likely to buy into a goal if they feel they were part of creating it
  • Feedback - constructive feedback must be provided regularly to maintain motivation; helps employees determine how well they are doing and which aspects of performance need to improve
  • Task complexity - goals need the right amount of complexity (mentally demanding, multiple steps, new thinking, high coordination); too complex = overwhelming and demotivating; too little = boring and demotivating; managers must choose complexity appropriate to employees' skills and knowledge
✔ Advantages
  • Simple and cost effective to implement
  • Easy for both managers and employees to understand
  • Can lead to better alignment of employee goals with business objectives
  • Can foster better relationships with employees
  • No uncertainty about which strategy to use to motivate people
✘ Disadvantages
  • Time consuming to negotiate goals with each employee in a large business
  • Providing ongoing feedback requires careful, accurate and detailed monitoring — also time consuming and potentially difficult to achieve
  • When two separate goals are set at the same time, focusing on one may make it difficult to achieve the other
  • There is only one strategy — goal setting
Motivation Theory

🔴Four Drive Theory (Lawrence and Nohria)

  • Published in 2002 by Paul Lawrence and Nitin Nohria
  • A framework to understand human behaviour; identifies four basic drives that result from our evolutionary past
  • Each drive influences interactions and outcomes in the workplace
  • Businesses typically focus on the drive to acquire (base pay, cash incentives), but the other three drives play an integral part in fully motivating employees
  • Acquire, bond, and learn are active drives — humans almost always seek to fulfil them; defend is latent — it must be stimulated by a threat to become active
The Four Drives

📊The Four Drives

  • Acquire - drive to acquire material needs (clothing, housing, money) and complex needs (status, power, influence); can lead to excellent performance but also detrimental competition
    • Manager strategies: reward system based on performance; career advancement opportunities; recognising contributions with achievement awards
  • Bond - drive to form relationships with individuals and groups; can moderate the drive to acquire and lessen unhealthy competition
    • Manager strategies: team-building sessions; social activities (e.g. playing soccer at lunch); showing genuine interest in and empathy for employees
  • Learn - drive to acquire knowledge, make sense of the world, fulfil curiosity, and learn new skills
    • Manager strategies: creating challenges and opportunities to learn and grow (e.g. setting challenging goals); ensuring jobs and tasks are interesting and meaningful
  • Defend - drive to protect ourselves, families, friends, beliefs and resources; threats to the individual, group, or business trigger this drive; can be a source of motivation if tempered and focused — harmful if over-stimulated
    • Manager strategies: eliminating or correcting information sources that communicate unintentional or misguided threats; providing employees with the means to respond to legitimate threats in the business's competitive or regulatory environment
Key Concept

⚖️Balance Between and Among Drives

  • The relative strength of each drive varies by individual and can vary over time within a single individual
  • It is detrimental when one drive dominates — leads to unbalanced personal and organisational outcomes
  • An important theme of the theory is balance between all four drives so they complement and regulate each other; structuring jobs to support this balance is an important workplace consideration
e.g. Acting purely on the drive to acquire can lead to destructive competition; over-stimulating the drive to defend can result in paranoia.
✔ Advantages
  • Any of the four drives can be used to motivate employees at any time — no specific order is required
  • A manager can identify which drives are dominant for specific employees and target those
✘ Disadvantages
  • Businesses need to provide opportunities to meet all four drives; if one or more are not met, employee motivation might decrease
  • Can be time consuming to identify the dominant drives for each individual employee

Motivation Strategies

Strategies managers use to motivate employees — including financial rewards, career growth, training, support, and formal sanctions.

Motivation Strategy

💵Performance-Related Pay (PRP)

  • System to motivate employees to achieve specific performance goals by giving financial rewards based on the performance of the individual, group, or business
  • Forms: bonus, commission, yearly pay rises, or shares
  • Employee receives a base salary; PRP is additional
  • Built on the assumption that rewards foster the right behaviour and that money is an effective incentive
  • Often used for higher management, salespeople, or manufacturing
Short- and long-term: More likely to have short-term benefits. If employees are not enjoying their work or gaining a sense of achievement, extra money may not be sufficient to motivate them in the long-term.
✔ Advantages
  • Top performers can be attracted and retained by businesses that recognise and reward performance
  • Can increase productivity levels
  • Cost efficient — PRP only paid when performance warrants it
  • Setting specific targets aligns employee objectives with those of the business
  • Sends a direct message that high performance gets rewarded
✘ Disadvantages
  • Can encourage wrong behaviour — employees focus excessively on reward-gaining (e.g. short-term gains over long-term, individual effort over teamwork, quantity over quality, unethical behaviour)
  • Money is not the only motivator; strategy does not account for different types of people
  • Factors outside the employee's control may impact outcomes, causing frustration and dissatisfaction
  • Different rewards between colleagues can create jealousy and mistrust
Motivation Strategy

📈Career Advancement

  • Reaching a higher position within a business, being allocated more responsibility in one's current role, or gaining experience in a different area of the business
  • Important element for employee motivation, satisfaction and retention
  • Most employees want to learn new skills and move up the career ladder, and are willing to invest effort and time to achieve this
  • Path varies by business and industry; employees must normally develop skills for the next-level position
  • Business can support employees through: training and development, mentor programs, opportunities to take on additional responsibilities, time off to study
Short- and long-term: More likely to have long-term motivational benefits. Employees need to demonstrate skills and work attitudes over an extended period before being considered for promotion.
✔ Advantages
  • Providing opportunities for career advancement can prevent talented workers from looking or being driven elsewhere
  • Opportunities for career advancement attract talented employees to the business
✘ Disadvantages
  • Employees who are overlooked for career advancement may feel de-motivated
  • Employees may be promoted beyond their level of capability
  • Competition for a position may create conflict within a working team
  • Businesses are constrained by their organisational structure, existing employee composition, and policies limiting the number or timing of promotions — managers may need to look at other strategies
Motivation Strategy

🎓Investment in Training

  • Training: the process of improving employees' skills and knowledge to help them perform their job more effectively and efficiently
  • Investment in training: the business funds the development of employees so that they learn new and improve existing skills
  • Employee skills and knowledge are a vital resource in a highly competitive global economy
  • Benefits both business and staff — increases employee performance and business productivity
  • Increases job satisfaction, morale and motivation; may enable employees to apply for higher or better paid roles
Short- and long-term: Both short- and long-term benefits are possible. Short-term: employee performance is likely to improve. Long-term: employees may be grateful for the opportunity and stay loyal to the business.
✔ Advantages
  • Increases productivity — employees perform job tasks better
  • Contributes to a positive corporate culture
  • Increases engagement, commitment, morale and motivation; decreases staff turnover
  • May increase customer satisfaction and returning customers; reduces complaints
  • Provides a sense of accomplishment; can fulfil the self-actualisation need (Maslow)
  • Consistent training ensures consistent practices and processes across the business
  • Helps build a workforce that adapts better to change
✘ Disadvantages
  • Can be expensive
  • De-motivating if employees are not given opportunities to use their new skills
  • Employees may leave for another business — loss of expertise and wasted training cost
  • Work time is lost during training, reducing productivity
  • Conflicts can arise if training is not accessible to all employees
Motivation Strategy

🤝Support Strategies

  • Provide assistance to employees whose motivation has been negatively impacted by problems at work or in their personal life
  • Possible strategies: advice and encouragement during difficult times; an employee assistance program providing confidential counselling
  • Support makes employees feel recognised and increases their interest in their work
  • Employees who feel valued and supported perform better and go beyond their required duties
  • Recommended that managers try to resolve issues through support before implementing formal sanctions
Short- and long-term: Can work as a short- or long-term motivator. For long-term effectiveness, ongoing support is usually necessary.
✔ Advantages
  • Low-cost strategy to increase motivation
  • Contributes to a positive corporate culture where employees feel valued
  • Can build confidence of employees who feel insecure or out of their depth
✘ Disadvantages
  • The manager might not know about an employee's problems, making it difficult to offer and provide support
  • Requires good interpersonal and communication skills from the manager, which might be lacking
Motivation Strategy

⚖️Sanction Strategies

  • Formal ways to manage unacceptable behaviour or unsatisfactory work performance by penalising an employee
  • Possible sanctions: reprimand (verbal or written), deductions or salary reduction, reassignment of duties, disciplinary counselling, termination of employment
  • Issues should be addressed promptly — the longer they continue, the more difficult and serious they become
  • Managers must analyse the situation carefully before using sanctions — there are many reasons why an employee may behave poorly or underperform (e.g. unclear goals or policies)
  • Sanctions are not appropriate if the employee is underperforming due to capability issues — consider investment in training instead
Short- and long-term: Positive impact on short-term motivation only. Not appropriate for ongoing use. The manager must address underlying causes so that sanctions are not needed again in the future.
✔ Advantages
  • A disciplinary policy provides a formal framework outlining steps to deal with unacceptable behaviour or unsatisfactory performance
  • Can motivate employees who respond well to discipline
✘ Disadvantages
  • Only impacts motivation in the short term
  • Employees may resent sanctions, causing their work performance to actually worsen
  • Excessive use can make employees feel threatened, increasing staff turnover

Training Options

Training can be delivered in the workplace (on-the-job) or away from it (off-the-job). Each approach has different methods, advantages, and disadvantages.

Training Type

🔧On-the-Job Training

Takes place in the real work environment. Often a one-to-one interaction. Can take a variety of forms:

  • Job coaching, tutoring/mentoring - provided by a supervisor or work colleague; may be instructions or directions to perform a particular task or function
  • Job rotation - employees are put on different jobs in turns so that they learn all sorts of tasks; aims to give comprehensive awareness of different departments
  • Apprenticeships - trainees serve as apprentices to experts for long periods to learn the functions of a job; often used in trades, crafts and technical fields requiring long training for proficiency
  • Internships - interns recruited to perform specific time-bound jobs or projects during their education; may be a requirement of their educational course
✔ Advantages
  • Performed directly in the context of the job — effective because it involves learning by experience, free from artificial classroom situations
  • Usually less expensive than off-the-job training, as it uses the actual workplace and other employees
  • Can be done during normal working hours
  • Can develop good working relationships between employees
✘ Disadvantages
  • The trainer may not be experienced enough to train others
  • As it is often informal, the training may not be systematically organised
  • Poorly conducted training may create safety hazards
  • May lead to distractions for normal work occurring
Training Type

🏫Off-the-Job Training

Conducted away from the workplace while employees are not working — at a specialist training centre, TAFE, or the company's own premises. Particularly useful for developing transferable skills usable across many parts of the business.

  • Seminars and workshops - instructor facilitates learning activities for a small group
  • Classroom lectures - verbal presentation by an instructor to a large audience; low cost per trainee, but often one-way communication with little interaction and no learning by practice
  • Computer-based training - courses delivered via software or the internet; flexible (trainees access at a time and pace that suits them), but less personal contact with the trainer and other trainees
✔ Advantages
  • Trainers are suitably experienced and qualified
  • Employees can access the latest information and technology
  • Training is systematically organised
  • Being away from the workplace allows trainees to concentrate more thoroughly on the training
  • Can provide formally recognised qualifications (e.g. Certificate IV)
✘ Disadvantages
  • Not always directly within the context of the job — often formal and more artificial in nature
  • Can be expensive — extra costs such as payments to training organisations; staff jobs may need to be covered by others, adding to costs
  • Can be time consuming, especially if employees need to travel to the training facility

Performance Management

Strategies used to evaluate and improve employee performance — including MBO, performance appraisals, self-evaluation, and employee observation.

Definition

📊Performance Management

  • Ongoing process of evaluating an employee's behaviours and actions
  • Analyses how effectively employees are fulfilling their responsibilities
  • Measures progress towards the achievement of individual and business objectives
Definition

🎯Performance Management Strategies

  • Processes used to evaluate and improve the performance of employees
  • Long-term plans of action used as part of a business's performance management system
  • Most businesses use a combination of strategies rather than one alone

🏆MBO

Manager and employee jointly set goals aligned to business objectives. Implemented in six steps.

📋Appraisals

Formal evaluation of an employee's work quality, productivity, skills and behaviours against pre-set targets.

🪞Self-Evaluation

Employee assesses their own performance against agreed criteria, often as a starting point for an appraisal.

👁️Employee Observation

Manager directly observes employee performance and provides feedback based on verifiable data.

Strategy

🏆Management by Objectives (MBO)

  • First outlined by Peter Drucker in 1954 in The Practice of Management
  • Manager and employee jointly agree on specific personal performance goals
  • Goals must be based on business objectives — aligns individual and business goals across all levels (business, departmental, individual)
  • All employees have a clear understanding of overall objectives and their contribution to achieving them
Link to Goal Setting Theory: MBO and Locke and Latham's Goal Setting Theory complement each other. Goal Setting Theory explains why goals motivate; MBO is the systematic whole-business application. Advantages and disadvantages of Goal Setting Theory therefore apply to MBO.
Process

🔄The Six-Step MBO Process

  • 1. Set Organisational Objectives - establish the overall goals of the business
  • 2. Cascade Objectives to Employees - break down business objectives to departmental and individual level
  • 3. Encourage Participation in Goal Setting - employees are involved in setting their own goals
  • 4. Monitor Progress - track whether employees are on track to meet goals
  • 5. Evaluate and Reward Performance - assess outcomes and recognise achievement
  • 6. Repeat the Cycle - the process is ongoing and continuous
Strategy

📋Performance Appraisals

Also known as a performance review or performance evaluation. Any method of evaluating an employee's work quality, productivity, skills, and behaviours against pre-set targets.

  • Determining how well employees have performed and whether objectives were achieved
  • Providing feedback
  • Establishing plans to improve performance (e.g. through training)
  • Setting objectives for the future
  • Reviving motivation
✔ Satisfactory Performance
  • Promotion
  • Pay rise
  • Bonus
✘ Unsatisfactory Performance
  • Demotion
  • Dismissal (being fired)
Strategy

🪞Self-Evaluation

  • Employees assess their own performance against previously agreed criteria (e.g. as part of the MBO process)
  • Often completed before a performance appraisal — forms the starting point for the manager-employee discussion
  • Engages employees by giving them an active role and a voice in the process
  • Helps employees prepare for the appraisal and suggest further training relevant to their role or career path
  • Gives the manager better insight into the employee's perspective — employee and manager perceptions of performance often differ
  • Can reveal vital information the manager would not otherwise have obtained
  • Helps identify training opportunities for employees
Strategy

👁️Employee Observation

  • Manager directly observes employee performance and provides feedback based on specific work-related behaviours and results
  • Feedback based on observed or verifiable data is more likely to influence behaviour than feedback that cannot be supported by firsthand information
Examples: Call centre calls recorded for training purposes; secret shoppers evaluating customer service; manager listening in to a retail conversation; salon owner observing an employee's haircut
Method

🔁360-Degree Feedback

  • Collects feedback from multiple sources — colleagues, subordinates, and customers — relating to an employee's performance
  • Provides a broader and more balanced picture than feedback from a single manager alone

Termination Management

Termination occurs when the working relationship between employer and employee ends. Businesses must manage the type of termination, employee and employer entitlements, and the transition period.

Termination Type

🏖️Retirement

  • Employee voluntarily leaves the workforce entirely — will not seek employment elsewhere
  • No mandatory retirement age in Australia; depends on personal circumstances and financial ability to retire
Termination Type

🚪Resignation

  • Employee voluntarily leaves the business (but not the workforce) — a personal decision
  • Reasons can include: accepting a position elsewhere with a promotion or better conditions; starting their own business; a change of lifestyle (e.g. moving from city to rural area); a change of career
Termination Type

📉Redundancy

Occurs when a position no longer exists due to:

  • Technological changes (e.g. job performed by a machine)
  • Staff reduction in response to a business slowdown
  • Business relocation interstate or overseas
  • Restructure or merger with another business
  • Business becoming insolvent or bankrupt
Voluntary Redundancy
  • Business offers redundancy packages; employees choose whether to accept
  • Attractive to employees near retirement or who can easily find another job
Involuntary Redundancy
  • Employment is terminated against the employee's will
Termination Type

⚠️Dismissal

Contract terminated due to non-satisfactory performance or serious misbehaviour.

  • Non-satisfactory performance: employee must be given notice and an opportunity to improve before dismissal can proceed
  • Serious misconduct: employee can be dismissed immediately — no notice required. Includes theft, assault, serious OHS breach, sexual harassment, or intoxication at work. Employer must: arrange a meeting, present the evidence, and allow the employee to respond to the allegations
  • Unfair dismissal: if dismissed in a harsh, unjust or unreasonable manner, employees can take legal action against their employer
Employee Rights

💰General Entitlements (All Termination Types)

Regardless of the type of termination, employees are entitled to be paid out:

  • Wages or salary still owing
  • Unused annual leave
  • Unused long-service leave — if they have worked for the business for at least 7 years (Victoria)
Note: Unused sick leave is not paid out.
Employee Rights

📜Additional Entitlements: Redundancy and Dismissal

  • Redundancy: employer usually must pay redundancy pay (based on length of service) and provide notice (length also based on service)
  • Dismissal — non-satisfactory performance: notice must be given and an opportunity to improve offered
  • Dismissal — serious misconduct: no notice required; employee can be dismissed immediately
Employer Rights

🏢Employer Entitlements

  • When an employee retires or resigns, the employer is entitled to be given notice that the employee is leaving
  • This allows time to find and recruit a replacement for the position
Employee Transition

👤Transition Considerations for Employees

  • Retirement: a major life change — business can support by offering counselling, providing superannuation or investment advice, or allowing a gradual transition (reduced hours before full retirement)
  • Resignation: employee remains in the workforce, so there are usually no transition issues to consider
  • Redundancy: can have a profound impact on mental wellbeing, family, and finances (e.g. loss of income, possible relocation, lifestyle change). Business can offer: references and recommendations, career or financial advice, counselling, training to increase employability, outplacement services (third-party specialist assistance to find new employment)
  • Dismissal: impact is often similar to redundancy; however, businesses usually do not offer transition support to dismissed employees
Employer Transition

🏢Transition Considerations for Employers

  • When an employee retires, resigns, or is dismissed — the business needs to consider succession planning and recruitment to fill the vacancy
  • This is not required when a position is made redundant, as the role no longer exists
  • It is recommended that employers conduct an exit interview when any employee leaves — to collect both positive and negative feedback from the departing employee

Key Elements of an Operations System

Every business has an operations system. All operations systems share three key elements: inputs, processes, and outputs.

Operations Management

⚙️What is an Operations System?

  • Every business — whether it produces goods or services — has an operations system
  • Without an operations system, a business could not achieve its objectives, as there would be no products or services to sell
  • All operations systems have three key elements: inputs, processes, and outputs

📥Inputs

The resources used in the production process — raw materials, buildings, human resources, and information.

🔄Processes

All activities used to turn inputs into outputs. This is where value is added and productivity gains are made.

📦Outputs

The final goods or services offered to customers. Quality is determined by the inputs used and transformation processes undertaken.

Operations Element

📥Inputs

Inputs are the resources used in the production process. These include:

  • Raw materials and components - unprocessed materials (e.g. iron ore, wood) and processed components/parts (e.g. processed steel, tyres)
  • Buildings, machinery and equipment - physical infrastructure used in production
  • Human resources - the work, knowledge and skills of employees
  • Information - knowledge about how to make a product (e.g. secret recipes) or about the market (e.g. trends)
Operations Element

🔄Processes

Processes are all the activities used to turn inputs into outputs. This is the point where value is added to the inputs and where productivity gains can be made through optimising production.

  • A labourer moulding steel into car parts
  • A chocolate manufacturer melting cocoa butter
  • A hairdresser doing a final check after cutting someone's hair
Key idea: Improving and optimising processes is a core way businesses increase productivity and efficiency.
Operations Element

📦Outputs

Outputs are the final goods or services that the business offers to its customers (e.g. a car or a haircut).

  • The quality of outputs is determined by the inputs used and the transformation processes undertaken
  • Both goods (physical products) and services (intangible results) can be outputs

Manufacturing versus Service Businesses

Operations management differs depending on whether a business produces physical goods or provides a service. Both types share the same fundamental operations structure.

Business Type

🏭Manufacturing Businesses

  • Tangible outputs - physical products that can be touched (e.g. cars, chocolate bars, computer hardware)
  • Stored and sold later - products can be produced in advance and held until sold (e.g. mobile phones stockpiled before release date; tinned fruit canned in season and shipped year-round)
  • Capital intensive - production requires high amounts of machinery and equipment
  • Minimal customer contact during the production process
  • Cannot be customised - outputs generally cannot be changed to suit individual customer preferences
Business Type

🤲Service Businesses

  • Intangible outputs - non-physical products that cannot be touched (e.g. a haircut, medical advice)
  • Cannot be stored and sold later - services must be consumed as they are provided
  • Labour intensive - production requires high amounts of human input and employees
  • Significant customer contact during provision - customers often consume the service while it is being delivered
  • Can be customised - services are often adapted to the wishes and preferences of individual customers
Note: A haircut is an intangible service — the hair itself can be touched, but the act of providing the haircut cannot.
Comparison

🔗Similarities Between Manufacturing and Service Businesses

  • Both use an operations system with inputs, processes and outputs
  • Both produce an output sold for money, allowing the business to make a profit
  • Both employ strategies to improve their operations system and increase efficiency
🏭 Manufacturing
  • Tangible (physical) outputs
  • Can be stored and sold later
  • Capital intensive
  • Minimal customer contact
  • Cannot be customised
🤲 Services
  • Intangible (non-physical) outputs
  • Cannot be stored and sold later
  • Labour intensive
  • Significant customer contact
  • Can be customised

Waste Management: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle

Strategies that improve efficiency and effectiveness of operations through waste minimisation in the production process, going above what is legally required.

Waste Management

♻️Reduce, Reuse, Recycle — Overview

  • 'Reduce, reuse, recycle' are strategies used to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of operations through waste minimisation in the production process
  • These strategies go above and beyond what is legally required — an example of acting in a socially responsible manner
  • Reduce — lower the amount of waste generated
  • Reuse — find ways to use items again rather than discarding them
  • Recycle — convert waste material into something new and useful instead of sending it to landfill
Waste Management

📉Reduce

  • Definition: lowering the amount of waste generated during operations
  • Improves efficiency by cutting unnecessary resource use, reducing costs
  • Improves effectiveness by demonstrating CSR, which can attract customers and improve reputation
e.g. A café uses washable cups, plates and cutlery instead of disposable items. Offices go paperless and print double-sided. Customers encouraged to bring their own shopping bags. Lights and electrical equipment switched off when not in use.
Waste Management

🔄Reuse

  • Definition: repeated use of items and finding ways to use things that would otherwise be thrown out
  • Improves efficiency by reducing spending on new materials, lowering input costs
  • Improves effectiveness by minimising waste in the production process, supporting CSR goals
e.g. Packaging materials (boxes, bubble wrap) that arrive with supplier inputs are reused when selling products to consumers, rather than purchasing new packaging.
Waste Management

🗑️Recycle

  • Definition: turning waste material into something new and useful instead of sending it to landfill
  • Improves efficiency by diverting waste that would otherwise incur disposal costs
  • Improves effectiveness by reducing environmental impact and supporting CSR, which can increase market share
e.g. Soft plastic packaging turned into picnic benches, playground equipment and recycling bins. Textiles (clothing, bedding) in poor condition converted into cleaning rags or insulation rather than sent to landfill.

Strategies Related to Technological Developments

Six technology-based strategies businesses use to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of their operations. Each can be evaluated by its advantages, disadvantages, and overall fit for a given scenario.

Technology Strategy

🏭Automated Production Lines

  • Definition: multiple workstations linked by a transfer system and electrical control system; each station performs a specific operation, with timing and sequencing controlled by computers
  • Best suited to high-volume (mass) production of goods with stable product designs — cannot accommodate frequent design changes
  • Employee input is reduced to designing systems, making adjustments, and supervising — not performing production tasks directly
  • Improves efficiency by speeding up production through repetitive sequencing and technology rather than human labour; economies of scale lower unit costs
  • Improves effectiveness by producing goods of consistent quality, satisfying customers and helping the business to make a profit
✔ Advantages
  • High production rate, reduced production times
  • Lower labour costs — less direct labour required
  • Lower unit costs — economies of scale from buying raw materials in bulk
  • Consistent quality; greater precision and accuracy
  • Occupational health and safety (OH&S) benefits — dangerous tasks performed by machines
  • Replaces repetitive and tedious tasks
✘ Disadvantages
  • High initial set-up costs; ongoing maintenance costs
  • Layout is relatively hard to change once built
  • CSR consideration: removal of human employment opportunity
Technology Strategy

🤖Robotics

  • Definition: the design, construction, operation and application of machines (robots) to perform tasks traditionally done by people
  • Used in manufacturing for repetitive, dangerous, or intricate tasks; often used within an automated production line
  • Also increasingly used to provide services (e.g. in hotels, hospitality)
  • Improves efficiency by speeding up the manufacturing process, reducing unit costs, and increasing accuracy — eliminating possible waste
  • Improves effectiveness by producing products of consistent quality, satisfying customers and helping the business make a profit and grow market share
✔ Advantages
  • Speeds up the manufacturing process
  • Increases accuracy, reduces errors
  • Can perform tasks that are dangerous for humans
  • Can work without needing breaks
✘ Disadvantages
  • Costly to set up (hardware, software, training)
  • Requires specialised knowledge and skills
  • Attracts ongoing maintenance costs
Technology Strategy

📐Computer-Aided Design (CAD)

  • Definition: computer software that enables engineers and architects to design and modify products, create precision drawings or technical illustrations, and document the design process
  • A standard tool for manufacturing and design businesses; used for new products, packaging, buildings, and product components
  • Improves efficiency by speeding up the product design process; allows the product to be tested virtually before production begins, reducing errors and eliminating possible waste
  • Improves effectiveness by calculating manufacturing costs during the design phase so changes can be made to maximise profit; 3D modelling lets clients request changes, increasing customer satisfaction and market share
✔ Advantages
  • Speeds up product design — faster to draw and modify than manual methods
  • Increases accuracy, reduces errors
  • Can test the product before starting production
  • Businesses and clients can collaborate on the design
  • 3D modelling provides clients with a realistic preview
✘ Disadvantages
  • Costly to set up (hardware, software, training)
  • Computers are prone to breakdowns, viruses and security breaches
  • Need to keep up with ever-changing and improving technology
Technology Strategy

⚙️Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM)

  • Definition: the use of software to control the manufacturing process — machinery, tools and equipment are operated via a computer
  • Often used after CAD: CAD drawings are linked to CAM machines to produce objects directly from designs
  • Widely used when a highly complex component or a large number of identical products are needed (e.g. automotive, electronics)
  • The process can be repeated to exact specifications — a level of precision impossible with hand-controlled tools
  • Improves efficiency by speeding up manufacturing and reducing unit costs; high accuracy eliminates wastage
  • Improves effectiveness by producing consistent quality products that satisfy customers, helping the business make a profit and grow market share
✔ Advantages
  • Speeds up manufacturing, reducing unit costs
  • High accuracy — less wastage, all products identical
  • Can manufacture complex parts that would be very difficult or impossible via manual machine control
✘ Disadvantages
  • High initial set-up costs, including employee training
  • Ongoing cost of staying up to date with new hardware and software
  • Time intensive to write the program to exact business and production requirements
Technology Strategy

🧠Artificial Intelligence (AI)

  • Definition: a range of smart technologies able to perform tasks that usually require human intelligence and that have the ability to learn and improve on their own
  • Can analyse data, detect patterns, and automate boring, repetitive, and time-consuming tasks
  • Improves efficiency by speeding up processes through technology rather than human labour, and automating operations to reduce costs
  • Improves effectiveness by producing goods and services of consistent quality, satisfying customers and helping the business make a profit
Manufacturing: increases accuracy and product quality, controls production rates, automates inventory forecasting and ordering.
Banking: detects fraud, processes simple loan applications, matches investment products to customers.
Chatbots: answer routine customer questions online, freeing staff for more complex tasks — reducing labour costs.
✔ Advantages
  • Drives innovation — uncovers opportunities for new products, services and business growth
  • Boosts efficiency through process automation
  • Increased precision and accuracy improves effectiveness
  • More consistent and higher quality outputs
  • Can reduce labour costs as staff are redeployed or replaced
  • Creates demand for highly skilled roles to maintain AI systems
✘ Disadvantages
  • Data trustworthiness concerns — individual rights at risk if data is lost, stolen or misused
  • Replaces human workers, removing jobs from employees who may lack the skills to keep pace
  • High cost of training existing workforce to operate AI systems
  • Every stage of AI implementation is costly: development, set-up, running, and ongoing maintenance all require significant investment and computing power
Technology Strategy

🌐Online Services

  • Definition: activities and processes that a business may offer over the internet — focus is on improving the operations system, not marketing or sales
  • Improves efficiency by enabling staff to be allocated where needed as demand arises; online bookings require fewer operators; reduces costs of running a physical shopfront
  • Improves effectiveness by providing greater business opportunities and competitiveness, increasing market share; convenience of online services can increase sales and profit
Study tip: Focus on how online services improve the operations system, not marketing and sales. Answers that only discuss marketing are not acceptable.
Examples

💡Examples of Online Services

  • Websites and mobile applications — customers order online, initiating the operations system; 'click and collect' allows optimal use of limited resources; production based on actual orders reduces overproduction and wastage
  • Booking systems — used in restaurants, hotels, hairdressers, medical appointments; allows businesses to allocate staff and resources accordingly
  • Customer feedback — via website or QR code; leads to changes in operations and improvements in product and service quality
  • Online training — self-paced via intranet or websites; increases staff knowledge and skills, leading to operations improvements
  • Cloud computing — remote servers store and process data; accessible anywhere, anytime; enables collaboration and speeds up processes
  • Website for suppliers — improves management of supplier relationships and communication, reducing delays in supply
✔ Advantages
  • Increased speed of delivery
  • Greater convenience to customers — services and bookings available online
  • Cheaper — reduced need for physical stores; reduced staffing
  • Quick and easy for customers to provide quality feedback
✘ Disadvantages
  • Initial cost of establishing a website can be expensive
  • Ongoing maintenance costs — particularly to ensure adequate security of customer data
  • Websites can crash, causing disruptions to the operations system
  • Delivery of physical goods purchased online adds costs and environmental impact from transportation

The Concept of Business Change

What change means in a business context, why it matters, and the two key approaches a business can take — proactive and reactive.

Key Concept

🔄Business Change

Change is a business's planned or unplanned response to internal or external pressures.
  • The ability to manage, adapt to, and embrace change will increasingly determine a business's competitive position and its future
  • Businesses that are not keeping up with change can fail
  • Change enables a business to meet the dynamic needs of its customers and create growth opportunities
  • Allows a business to keep up with advancing technology and respond to different economic conditions (e.g. strong or weak economic growth)
Approach to Change

🔭Proactive Approach

A proactive approach is where the business initiates change before the need for it arises.

This can be achieved through:

  • Anticipating future driving forces
  • Forecasting possible future events
  • Analysing key performance indicators (KPIs) to identify trends and pressures early
  • Being innovative and investing in research and development
✔ Advantages
  • Businesses that pursue this approach are generally more successful than those that do not
  • Allows the business to gain and maintain a competitive advantage
  • Opportunities and problems can be identified early
  • Time to analyse all factors and plan the change carefully
✘ Disadvantages
  • Requires ongoing investment into research and preparation for possible (and uncertain) future scenarios
Approach to Change

⚡Reactive Approach

A reactive approach is when the business is forced to act on internal or external pressures.

This can take the form of:

  • Responding to driving forces
  • Observing and imitating actions of competitors
  • Responding after a crisis has arrived
✔ Advantages
  • Easier to identify the required change — the problem or pressure is already visible
  • Imitating competitors allows the business to copy only the strategies that have proven successful
✘ Disadvantages
  • Risk of being too late to respond, especially in today's highly competitive markets where staying ahead is critical
  • Businesses are often unprepared, forcing changes in a limited time and resulting in rushed and potentially incorrect decisions
Unit 4 · Week 1 · Lesson 2

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

The 10 KPIs from the Study Design — what each measures, the objective it links to, and how it can drive and inform change.

Key Concept

📊What is a KPI?

A key performance indicator (KPI) is a tool that managers use to analyse and evaluate a business's progress towards its objectives.
  • KPIs measure efficiency (how well a business uses its resources) and effectiveness (the extent to which a business achieves its stated objectives)
  • They provide data that can drive and inform change
  • Before change can happen, managers must understand where the business currently stands and compare results against desired objectives
  • KPIs can also be compared over time or against industry benchmarks — this is called benchmarking
Study Design

📋The 10 KPIs You Must Know

  • Percentage of market share
  • Net profit figures
  • Rate of productivity growth
  • Number of sales
  • Rates of staff absenteeism
  • Level of staff turnover
  • Level of wastage
  • Number of customer complaints
  • Number of website hits
  • Number of workplace accidents
Study tip: Use precise terminology. The KPI is 'percentage of market share', not 'market share'. You must also be able to justify why a chosen KPI is appropriate for a given situation.
Framework

🔗Objectives → Strategies → KPIs

  • Set objectives — what are we aiming to achieve?
  • Develop strategies — how will we achieve the objectives?
  • Evaluate performance through KPIs — have we achieved the objectives?
KPI

🥧Percentage of Market Share

A business's portion of an industry's total sales, expressed as a percentage.
  • Links to the objective: to increase market share
  • An increase indicates the business is performing successfully
  • A decrease means customers are choosing competitors — suggests the business may be losing its competitive advantage (e.g. dissatisfied customers, changes in consumer behaviour, new competitors)
KPI

💰Net Profit Figures

Net profit is the difference between revenue and all expenses — direct costs (materials, shipping, wages related to production) and general/administrative expenses (rent, insurance, advertising, salaries not directly tied to production).
  • Links to the objective: to make a profit
  • Also relates to: to meet shareholder expectations — shareholders monitor this KPI as net profits may be paid as dividends
  • Low, negative, or decreasing net profit may indicate difficulties: decreased sales, increased expenses, poor customer service, or unmotivated employees
KPI

📈Rate of Productivity Growth

An increase in the value of outputs produced for a given level of inputs, over a given period of time.
  • Productivity = output per unit of input; measures efficiency of the operations system
  • Two ways to increase productivity: decrease inputs or increase outputs (e.g. reducing wastage, producing faster)
  • A positive rate = lower cost per unit → either increased profit or lower prices for consumers → greater competitiveness
  • Analysed regularly to identify and address production problems early and monitor competitiveness
KPI

🛒Number of Sales

The total quantity of goods or services sold within a certain timeframe.
  • Indicates whether a business is meeting sales objectives and forecasts
  • Used to evaluate the performance of marketing strategies
  • Businesses aim to increase sales even if short-term profits fall due to higher marketing costs, expecting higher long-term profits
  • A decrease suggests difficulties such as changes in consumer behaviour or new competitors
KPI

🪑Rates of Staff Absenteeism

The number of days employees are absent on days they are scheduled to work.
  • Includes sick leave and personal leave; does not include annual leave or public holidays
  • Causes lost productivity, higher costs (replacement staff), and lower profits
  • A high rate may indicate job dissatisfaction, stress, burnout, or poor corporate culture
  • Individual employees with high absenteeism may be facing personal difficulties — managers should look at how to provide support
KPI

🚪Level of Staff Turnover

The number of employees leaving a business in a specific timeframe who need to be replaced.
  • Employees made redundant do not count
  • High turnover results in recruiting and training costs, and loss of productivity and knowledge
  • An indicator of staff satisfaction — a high or increasing level indicates issues (e.g. dissatisfaction with training, pay, or working conditions)
  • The cause should be identified and addressed to reduce turnover
KPI

🗑️Level of Wastage

The number of products or materials discarded during the production process.
  • Causes: low quality inputs, inefficient processes, poorly trained employees
  • Wastage costs money — the business paid for materials that were then discarded
  • Businesses aim to reduce wastage to cut production costs and increase competitiveness
  • Also a CSR consideration — reducing wastage lessens environmental impact
  • A high level indicates issues that need to be identified and addressed
KPI

📞Number of Customer Complaints

The number of customers who have reported to the organisation that they are dissatisfied with goods or services provided.
  • An indicator of how customers view the business and the quality of its product or service
  • Common causes: faulty products, incorrect descriptions, poor customer service, slow delivery
  • High or increasing complaints can damage reputation and impact future sales
  • May indicate a need for additional staff training if poor customer service is the cause
KPI

🌐Number of Website Hits

How many visitors a business's website receives in a given period.
  • An indicator of customer interest and how attractive the website is to potential customers
  • A low number may indicate: customers are not interested in the offering, the website does not meet customer needs, or the site is not optimised for search engines
KPI

⚠️Number of Workplace Accidents

The number of people injured at work over a period of time.
  • Accidents can stop or slow production; workers who feel unsafe are often unmotivated or may leave the business
  • Avoidable accidents are costly through compensation payments and negatively impact reputation
  • Causes need to be identified and addressed immediately

Force Field Analysis Theory (Lewin)

A change management tool developed by Kurt Lewin to identify and weigh the forces for and against change.

Theory

⚖️Force Field Analysis

A change management tool used to better understand, plan and manage change processes.
  • Developed by Kurt Lewin in the 1940s to identify and analyse the pressures for and against change
  • Two types of forces have an impact on change: driving forces and restraining forces

🔼Driving Forces

Pressures or factors that initiate, encourage and support the change in the desired direction.

🔽Restraining Forces

Forces in favour of the status quo - they work against and resist the change.

Key Concept

🧮Equilibrium

When driving and restraining forces are in equal balance, there is no change.
  • Managers trying to implement change conduct a Force Field Analysis to identify the factors that must be addressed and monitored for the change to succeed
Process

📋Four Key Principles

  • Weighting - all driving and restraining forces are identified and rated on a scale, usually 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), with the total of each side tallied
  • Ranking - forces are placed in order, starting with the strongest. Lets the manager identify which restraining forces need to be eliminated or weakened, and which driving forces can be strengthened or added
  • Implementing a response - carrying out the actual change. Success is more likely when driving forces are stronger than restraining forces, so this means strengthening driving forces, reducing/eliminating restraining forces, and/or executing the planned change
  • Evaluating the response - managers monitor progress and analyse data to draw conclusions, modifying strategies if the change isn't tracking as planned, and comparing the outcome to what the analysis predicted

Driving and Restraining Forces

The pressures that push a business towards change, and the pressures that work to keep it at the status quo.

Driving forces initiate, encourage and support change in the desired direction.

🧑‍💼Owners

  • Want the business to meet objectives (e.g. profit, market share) - livelihood often depends on it
  • Drive change to anticipate and respond to the business environment

📊Managers

  • Responsible for efficient, effective operations
  • Use KPIs to review performance and plan changes
  • Often involved in implementing the change too

👥Employees

  • Critical to success - support and satisfaction drive a successful change process
  • Can drive change with their ideas/experience, if encouraged to share them
  • Unmet needs (pay, conditions, job security) may lead to industrial action, forcing change

⚔️Competitors

  • Must monitor rivals' strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and mistakes
  • e.g. new entrants, competitor pricing, new products, new distribution channels (e.g. online shopping)
  • Businesses aim to lead the competition, not just follow it

⚖️Legislation

  • New or amended laws (federal, state, local) force compliance
  • e.g. taxation, anti-discrimination, privacy laws

💰Pursuit of Profit

  • Profit is a fundamental business objective (see Unit 3, Week 1)
  • Missing profit targets forces a review of revenue/costs
  • Shareholders pressure for higher profit (share price, dividends)

✂️Reduction of Costs

  • Lower costs improve profit and allow lower, more competitive prices
  • e.g. cheaper suppliers, reduced wages (outsourcing/automation), cheaper utilities, optimised processes

🌍Globalisation

  • Increasing interdependence between countries - free trade and capital movement
  • Internet and lower transport costs remove boundaries to global markets
  • Forces businesses to compete nationally and internationally, not just locally

⚙️Technology

  • Machinery/equipment from scientific knowledge - improves efficiency and productivity
  • Businesses must keep up with advances to stay competitive

💡Innovation

  • Creating new ideas, or improving something already established
  • Key question: "how can we make this better?" - applies to products, services or processes
  • Positions a business ahead of its competitors

🌐Societal Attitudes

  • Businesses must respond to changing opinions, values and lifestyles
  • e.g. social media use, an ageing population shifting demand, CSR expectations, demand for work-life balance
Restraining forces are in favour of the status quo - they work against and resist change.

📊Managers

  • May not support change if it threatens their position (management roles often cut to reduce costs)
  • May resist if they lack the skills/experience to implement it successfully
  • Hasty or poorly communicated decisions, or indecisiveness, can undermine change and employee confidence

👥Employees

  • Fear of losing their job, especially if only some roles are threatened - creates mistrust
  • Fear of the unknown - loss of identity, or fear of failing in altered roles
  • Employees who feel uninvolved or unappreciated are more likely to resist

⏱️Time

  • Not enough time to plan carefully can make change rushed, or poorly timed
  • Lagging behind competitors (not foreseeing change) leaves insufficient time to act

🐢Organisational Inertia

  • The tendency of a business to do nothing and maintain its status quo
  • Indicates how fast/slow a business reacts - newer businesses are usually more dynamic than older, established ones

⚖️Legislation

  • Restricts operational practices, e.g. environmental laws, competition laws (prevent mergers/monopolies), council regulations
  • Acts as a stronger restraint if changes are unexpected, costly or difficult to implement

💵Financial Considerations

  • Costs of change: new technology/equipment, retraining, recruiting, redundancy payments
  • High costs may force price rises, hurting competitiveness/sales - especially if competitors face less pressure
  • Lack of access to finance is also a restraint, especially for small businesses

Porter's Generic Strategies

Three basic strategic options for gaining competitive advantage within an industry, as developed by Michael Porter in the 1980s.

Theory

🎯Porter's Generic Strategies

  • Developed by Michael Porter in the 1980s to explain how businesses create and sustain a competitive advantage
  • Competitive advantage = the 'edge' that wins sales from competitors
  • Three basic strategic options for gaining competitive advantage in an industry

📋The Three Strategies

  • Cost leadership - minimising costs, with the option of prices lower than competitors' for equivalent goods/services
  • Differentiation - being unique in attributes valued by consumers
  • Focus - offering a specialised product/service in a niche market
This course only covers cost leadership (lower cost) and differentiation, as per the Study Design.
e.g. Grocery shopping - ALDI/Costco cut costs to a minimum and pass savings on as lower prices to grab market share. Coles/Woolworths offer more products and better service, with higher prices covering their higher costs.
Strategy

💰Lower Cost (Cost Leadership)

  • Being the leader in terms of cost in an industry/market
  • About minimising the cost of producing/delivering products and services
  • The price paid by the customer is a separate issue

📈How Lowest-Cost Businesses Use This

  • Increase market share by charging lower prices, still making a reasonable profit per sale (e.g. ALDI, Kmart)
  • Increase profits by reducing costs while charging industry-average prices

✂️Ways to Reduce Costs

  • Reducing operating costs - e.g. less staff (wages), less stock
  • Controlling the supply chain - Just in Time (JIT), squeezing suppliers on price
✔ Advantages
  • Attracts price-conscious customers
  • Efficiency emphasis helps withstand price competition from rivals
  • Discourages new businesses from entering - hard to undercut the cost leader's prices
✘ Disadvantages
  • Risk of damaging price wars with competitors
  • Lower cost focus can decrease product quality - lower sales, more complaints
  • Needs high sales volume for sufficient profit - requires significant upfront investment
  • Cutting market research/R&D/advertising to save costs can hurt ability to detect and lead change
Strategy

✨Differentiation

  • Distinguishing a product/service from others to gain competitive advantage
  • The business seeks to be unique in attributes valued by consumers
  • Exact method depends on the industry and the product/service

🛠️Common Differentiation Features

  • Superior product quality - features, functionality, durability, reliability
  • Innovation - being first to offer the product/service
  • Excellent customer support - e.g. additional warranties
  • Brand image - strong recognition and desire, brand loyalty
  • Wide availability - product stocked by many retailers
Price premium from differentiating must exceed the cost of differentiating, otherwise the strategy isn't profitable.
e.g. Apple, Dyson, and luxury brands
✔ Advantages
  • Ability to obtain premium prices from customers
  • Creates buyer loyalty - loyal customers are not price sensitive and unlikely to switch
  • Brand loyalty can deter potential new market entrants
✘ Disadvantages
  • Often involves higher costs
  • Possible imitation by competitors - forces constant reinvention
  • Customers may not pay extra for unique features, opting for cheaper alternatives
  • Can take time for consumers to realise the product is better than competitors'
