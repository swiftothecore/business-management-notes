10Global strategies — keep the three separateGlobal
Sourcing
Global sourcing of inputs — obtaining raw materials and parts from suppliers outside the home country. Higher quality inputs → fewer complaints; cheaper inputs → sales, market share, net profit. Access to resources, technology and knowledge unavailable domestically.
Overseas mfr
Overseas manufacture — production of the good is located in a different country to headquarters. Lower labour/production costs → net profit; countries specialising in an industry → quality up, complaints down.
Outsource
Global outsourcing — transferring business functions to external overseas businesses. Cuts costs and/or lifts quality → net profit, sales, market share, productivity growth, complaints.
Trap: sourcing buys inputs, overseas manufacture relocates production the business still owns, outsourcing hands a function to another business. Marks are lost every year on this.

C

Developing a positive corporate culture

Week 7 · L1
Recruitment
Select for fit with the desired culture, not just skills — interviews and reference checks test values.
Training
Explicitly reinforces desired values (e.g. teamwork); induction includes a culture section.
Communication
Values reinforced via meetings, emails, newsletters and the website; two-way, so employees feel valued.
Policies
Mission and vision statements, a code of conduct, dress code — set standards of behaviour.
Rewards
Reward what you want repeated: team rewards → collaboration; rewarding ideas → innovation.
Rituals & celebrations
Team lunches build belonging; 'heroes' (employee of the month) model the values.
Changing management styles
Autocratic blocks contribution and breeds resentment; consultative empowers employees and widens the knowledge used in decisions.
D

The learning organisation — Senge

Week 7 · L2
In rapid change only flexible, adaptive and productive businesses succeed, so they become learning organisations that facilitate members' ongoing growth. Five disciplines = practices to be studied and implemented.
I
Personal mastery
A mindset and spiritual attitude, not a level of proficiency and not a status to reach — a lifelong discipline; high-mastery staff take initiative and learn faster.
II
Mental models
Ingrained assumptions and generalisations shaping how we see and act — dangerous when incorrect or unexamined. Replace confrontation with inquiry and trust.
III
Shared vision
A common understanding of a desired future, genuine and built from employees' own visions — not imposed. People then learn because they want to.
IV
Team learning
Collaboration building the team's capacity — teams, not individuals, are the fundamental learning unit; supported by knowledge management.
V
Systems thinking
See the business as a whole and the links between its parts — patterns, not isolated events. New machinery forces changes in HR training and finance.
E

Effect of change on stakeholders

Week 8 · L1
StakeholderPositiveNegative
Owners Better performance → higher return on investment, e.g. rising share price. Failure costs partners the business and shareholders their share value; dissenters sell.
Managers Bonuses or performance-related pay; advancement in a restructure. Stress from delivering bad news and facing resistance; middle-management roles cut; replaced if it fails.
Employees Advancement and new skills; if consulted, motivation rises. New skill demands feel threatening; changed hours, location or redundancy hit finances and wellbeing.
Customers Better quality, lower prices, products that suit them better. Prices may rise, or the product changes in a way they don't support.
Suppliers More inputs required → increased demand and sales. Fewer inputs, or a switch of supplier, cuts sales; new specs force costly adaptation.
Community Expansion creates jobs and lifts local foot traffic. Closures cut local incomes and spending — devastating when a small town loses a major employer.
Framing line: change benefits the business overall but alters the status quo — managers must minimise the negative impact.
F

Overcoming employee resistance

Week 8 · L2
Resistance to change: any attitude or behaviour showing unwillingness to make or support a desired change. Leads to falling output, resignations, hostility to change leaders, industrial action, and negative talk that sways co-workers.
Low risk More likely to win genuine support; effective short-term and more so long-term — employees feel secure and motivated.

Communication

Transferring information about the change to employees — assumes that understanding it produces support. Open, honest and two-way: email, one-to-one, reports, presentations.
+ Cost effective — uses existing channels.
– Time consuming with a large workforce.

Empowerment

Giving employees authority to make decisions and help design the change — people resist less what they helped decide. Usually shapes how it is implemented, not whether it happens.
+ Brings new and fresh ideas.
– Differing ideas cause conflict; slows decisions.

Support

Assistance through the change: training for new roles, redeployment, outplacement or counselling for those made redundant, and time to adjust.
+ Employees feel valued; builds culture.
– Time consuming and expensive.

Incentives

Benefits offered in exchange for supporting the change — promotions, higher wages or bonuses, better conditions.
+ Positive association; employees feel valued.
– Support is bought, not believed; expensive.
High risk Quick short-term, but a greater chance of negative outcomes — conflict and damage to the manager–employee relationship.

Manipulation

Selectively withholding or altering information so the change looks more favourable or necessary — e.g. promoting the training opportunities of new technology while concealing forced redundancies.
+ Quick and cheap when nothing else will work.
– If discovered: more resistance, damaged culture.

Threat

Using negative consequences — loss of promotion, transfer, overtime, entitlements or termination — to force compliance when a manager won't invest time in other strategies.
+ Produces the desired behaviour quickly, through fear.
– Compliance masks resentment → sabotage, grievances, disputes.
G

Corporate social responsibility in change

Week 8 · L1
Stakeholders expect businesses to contribute to the economic, social and environmental welfare of their communities; reputation, investment and consumer spending follow CSR performance.
New technology
Ensure it doesn't harm the environment; train staff to reduce stress and anxiety.
Redundancy
Resume support, career counselling, and honesty about the change and its impact.
Change of supplier
Use local suppliers where possible; if overseas, ensure workers are not exploited, are paid fairly, and have decent conditions.
Exam technique: CSR depends on the case study. Ask what the change does to the environment, to society/the community, and to employees and their families.
H

Lewin's three-step change model — and closing the loop

Week 9 · L1–L2
Step 1
Unfreezing
Create awareness that the status quo is hindering the business, since people naturally resist change. Old behaviours, processes and structures are examined to show why change is necessary. Communication is critical: the more employees grasp the logic, benefit and urgency, the more motivated they are to accept it.
Step 2
Changing
The business transitions into the new state and the change becomes real. The hardest step, marked by uncertainty and fear. Employees learn new behaviours and processes, so education, communication, support and time are essential, alongside reminders of the reasons and benefits.
Step 3
Refreezing
Reinforce and solidify the new state as the norm so people don't revert. Rewards and acknowledgement of individual effort make the new behaviour repeat. Some call refreezing outdated given constant change — but skipping it risks "one step forward, two steps back".
Then start again
Reviewing KPIs
The business re-analyses the same KPIs to judge whether the change succeeded or needs further modification — the final key knowledge point of Unit 4.
Louisa's Café, Coburg — sales down, complaints up, cause traced to coffee quality → new machine plus staff training, targets sales +20% and complaints −50% → after three months sales +23%, complaints −55%: change effective.
Command words: Outline = brief statement of main features · Explain = make clear, with reasons and links · Analyse = break into parts, show relationships · Discuss = both sides · Evaluate / Justify = both sides plus a stated decision.
